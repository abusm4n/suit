\begin{abstract}
Software updates are how Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices receive security fixes, yet little is known about how they fetch updates over the network and how well that delivery is protected.
We record the traffic of ten consumer devices while they update and re-analyze a large public dataset of IoT traffic recorded years earlier.
We first separate update traffic from everything else a device sends, using update servers confirmed by hand, and show that simple keyword matching mostly selects unrelated traffic.
We then examine each update channel together with the protection inside the firmware image it delivers.
Three of the seven devices that downloaded an update received the image unencrypted, two from vendor servers and one from a desktop application on the home network; signatures that reveal tampering were found only in the first two images.
Encrypted channels mostly use strong settings; some vendors still use outdated ones, and the legacy options devices offer mainly reflect software defaults.
Certificates from public authorities are short-lived, whereas vendors' own authorities issue certificates valid for a decade or more, one with a 1024-bit key\textcolor{blue}{, and devices never checked these for revocation}.
We map each weakness to the attacks it enables and what they require.
