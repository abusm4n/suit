\paragraph{Devices used}

%evaluated.

The table reports the current firmware version detected on each device and the updated version installed during the experiment. 
Three (sony-tv, fire-tv, and d-link-cam) of the ten devices were already up to date at the time of testing; for these devices, we checked update availability and recorded the resulting network traffic.
The \texttt{packets} column shows the total number of packets captured during each update session, showing variability across devices. 
For example, apple-tv (1,405,566 packets) and homepod (767,196 packets) generated significantly higher traffic volumes, likely due to large firmware downloads and/or higher gap version numbers. The remaining devices exhibited moderate traffic volumes, ranging from a few thousand packets (e.g., d-link-cam, fire-tv, tapo-c100) to tens of thousands (e.g., eufy, reolink, tapo-c200).

\subsubsection{Retrospective Experiments}
Across the 34,586 experiments reported in \cite{10.1145/3355369.3355577}, we identified $6,315$ update-related traffic flows. 
106  of these flows were observed in encrypted channels, $2,415$ were observed in cleartext, and the $3,794$ remainder had undetermined encryption status.
%Encrypted update traffic is rare: in the retrospective dataset we found 106 encrypted update flows comprising
In the $106$ encrypted flows, $71$ occurrences of the token \texttt{update}, $32$ of \texttt{software}, and $3$ of \texttt{download}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap-encrypted}.
By contrast, the $2,415$ unencrypted update traffic contained $1,569$ occurrences of \texttt{update}, $735$ of \texttt{firmware}, $89$ of \texttt{software}, and $22$ of \texttt{download}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap-unencrypted}.
%Figure~\ref{fig:update_related_traffic} shows two heatmaps that summarize keyword occurrences by device and encryption status: subfigure (a) visualizes the 106 encrypted update flows, and subfigure (b) visualizes the 2,415 unencrypted update flows. 
Matches in the encrypted subset are spread fairly evenly across TV and audio devices (4--11 per device), whereas the unencrypted subset is dominated by \texttt{wemo-plug} (1{,}205) and \texttt{philips-hub} (509), followed by \texttt{samsung-tv} (250); per-device counts are given in Figure~\ref{fig:update_related_traffic}.
%Some devices generate update-related traffic while idle, indicating background update checks or periodic polling.
In this setup, negotiated sessions are predominantly TLS 1.2,  with no observed TLS 1.3 negotiation.
Based on this result, we selected the top ten update-related devices for comparison in our controlled experiments.

\subsection{Payload Analysis (RQ1b)}

Instead of counting packet, per packet, we used STREAM, and a stream In network traffic analysis, a stream (often called a flow) refers to a sequence of packets that belong to the same continuous communication between two endpoints.
All packets that share the same 5-tuple:
source IP, destination IP, Source port, Destination port, Protocol (TCP/UDP).

This grouping represents one direction or bidirectional exchange between two devices.
Note that we TCP stream, which  includes ALL protocols (including the ones reported in table Protocols) on top of TCP.

The TCP stream numbers are assigned strictly based on the order in which new TCP connections appear in the capture.

Since we do not have ground truth that an actual firmware or software update occurred for every session, we extracted the payload size of update-related traffic and used it as a primary heuristic to infer likely update transfers. Payload length was computed by extracting the raw hexadecimal payload string from each packet (e.g., \emph{tcp\_tcp\_payload\_raw} or f\emph{rame\_raw} for TCP/UDP, respectively). 
Because each byte is represented by two hex characters, the payload byte count is obtained by halving the string length; this byte count is recorded as the session payload length in the output data. The per-session payload sizes are plotted in Figure \ref{fig:update_related_traffic_payload}.

This figure shows clear variation in payload size both across and within device classes. 
The overall mean payload size is approximately 575 bytes, while the largest observed single session is 3,260 bytes (fire-tv). 
Most sessions cluster in the low-hundreds byte range and therefore appear to be short control/metadata exchanges (for example, version checks or artifacts).
A smaller subset of sessions reaches kilobyte scale; these large transfers are the primary candidates for actual update downloads.

\begin{comment}
    
\noindent\textbf{Authenticity and Confidentiality.}
Before assessing encryption quality and cryptographic strength, we note that both properties are predicated on authenticity: a device must first verify that it is communicating with a legitimate update server before any form of encryption can offer meaningful protection.
Firmware confidentiality is equally critical. The firmware binary itself is sensitive data: an adversary who can intercept an unencrypted or weakly encrypted update stream obtains the binary, which can be reverse-engineered to identify unpatched vulnerabilities, extract cryptographic keys or credentials embedded in the image, and reconstruct the exact version history of the device.
Knowledge of the precise firmware version further enables \emph{downgrade attacks}: by suppressing the delivery of a newer, patched update and replaying an older, vulnerable firmware image, an adversary can persistently maintain a device in an exploitable state.
Server authentication in TLS relies on X.509 certificate validation (analyzed in \S\ref{subsec:cert}); the entropy and cipher suite analyses that follow characterize how well the channel protects firmware confidentiality once server identity is established.
\end{comment}

\subsection{Entropy Analysis (RQ2a)}









In our analysis, we computed the \texttt{Shannon, Rényi}, and \texttt{Tsallis} entropy for the update-related traffic. 
A normalized Shannon entropy value approaching 1.0 indicates maximum randomness, consistent with encrypted or compressed data, while low values indicate structured or plaintext payloads. Following prior work~\cite{10.1145/3355369.3355577}, we use a threshold of $H_{\text{Shannon}} \geq 0.80$ as an indicator of likely encryption; the study in \cite{10.1145/3355369.3355577}  reports $H_{enc} = 0.85$ for encrypted and $H_{uenc} = 0.25$ for plaintext payloads across their full dataset, providing a reference baseline.

The  mean entropy ($H$) for the ten devices is 0.78 for Shannon,
0.74, for Rényi, and 0.95 for Tsallis.
Figure \ref{fig:entropy_heatmap} highlights the mean entropy values (± standard deviation).
High-entropy devices are apple-tv, %(0.88),

Overall, this analysis exposes a clear encryption gap across devices, with low-entropy devices transmitting significant plaintext and firmware binaries recoverable from the update traffic from certain devices.

\subsubsection{Retrospective experiments}
An entropy ($H$) analysis was conducted in \cite{10.1145/3355369.3355577}, reporting an average of $H_{enc}=0.85$ for encrypted traffic payloads and $H_{uenc}=0.25$ for unencrypted traffic payloads. 
However, their analysis was not focused on update-related traffic; instead, it covered the overall experiments. Moreover, the authors did not specify which entropy metric was applied. 
Upon reviewing their methodology, we inferred that only Shannon entropy was used in their analysis.
The  mean entropy ($H$) in our analysis for the ten devices is 0.71 for Shannon,
0.65, for Rényi, and 0.93 for Tsallis.
Figure~\ref{fig:entropy_heatmap_re} highlights the mean entropy values (± standard deviation).
Similar to the controlled experiments, apple-tv is among the highest-entropy devices, second only to fire-tv (Shannon 0.82 vs.\ 0.86).
Figure~\ref{fig:entropy_boxplot_re} presents the per-device distribution.

%not as a measure of cryptographic strength, 
so we read it against the baseline reported in \cite{10.1145/3355369.3355577}.
Across the $6{,}113$ update-related captures with a computable payload (of $6{,}315$), the mean per-capture Shannon entropy is 0.71;

\subsection{Certificate Security Analysis (RQ2c)}
\label{subsec:cert}






















%%\textbf{Certificate Analysis:}
To complement the cipher suite evaluation, we analyzed the X.509 certificates exchanged during TLS handshakes in the controlled experiments. 
While cipher suites govern the session-level encryption, certificates underpin the trust model: a weak certificate key can, in principle, be factored to impersonate an update server regardless of cipher suite strength, a present-day concern for the small $\leq$80-bit fraction we observe, whereas the 112-bit majority is exposed instead by the looming 2031 transition rather than by current factoring.
Across the ten devices, we extracted 94 unique certificates from seven IoT devices.
%(d-link-cam, homepod, and reolink had no extractable certificate data from their update traffic).
% shorten

The certificate ecosystem is highly fragmented: Apple devices exclusively use Apple-issued certificates, Amazon devices rely on Amazon and DigiCert CAs, while other devices use a mix of self-signed (SS)  CAs and public CAs (DigiCert, GeoTrust).
Notably, some devices, such as fire-tv contact a wide range of certificate authorities (15 unique issuers), reflecting the diversity of backend services contacted during updates, including Netflix, Spotify, and Google endpoints.
