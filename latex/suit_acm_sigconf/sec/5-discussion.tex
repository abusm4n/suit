\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Update process characteristics (RQ1)}
Update delivery relies on third-party cloud and CDN infrastructure spanning multiple countries, over TCP and primarily TLS~1.2.
Retrospectively, 38.2\% of update-related flows were plaintext and 60.1\% undetermined, leaving only a small fraction confidently encrypted.
These are upper bounds on update-related traffic, limited by keyword-filter precision (limitation (i)); even so, substantial cleartext on the update paths of privacy-sensitive devices points to a recurring supply-chain gap rather than an isolated case.

\paragraph{Encryption and cryptographic strength (RQ2)}
Authenticity is the precondition for both properties: three devices exposed no certificate at all, and five rely at least partly on self-signed certificates, so entropy and cipher strength are moot for those sessions until server identity is established.
Entropy averages 0.78 (controlled) and 0.71 (retrospective), below both the 0.80 threshold and the $H_{enc}=0.85$ baseline for general encrypted IoT traffic~\cite{10.1145/3355369.3355577}.
Insecure and weak suites dominate what clients \emph{offer} (78.2\%) but not what servers \emph{negotiate} (97.0\% secure or recommended), so the realized exposure is to downgrade and to the residual devices that negotiate weak suites outright, not to pervasive weak encryption.
Certificates average 115.4-bit strength (19.1\% at $\geq$128~bits) with 8.74-year validity.
That average is not itself a vulnerability, but 46.8\% of validity windows exceed five years, and IoT devices rarely rotate keys or honor revocation~\cite{10.1145/2815675.2815685,10710264}, so a non-trivial share will plausibly still be deployed when 112-bit strength is disallowed in 2031, with no mechanism to replace them.
Against the 2019 traces (limitation (ii)), this posture is improving rather than static: TLS~1.3 and stronger certificates appear only in the controlled set.

\paragraph{Vulnerability implications (RQ3)}
Mapping the observed insecure and weak configurations to public vulnerability records shows that these weaknesses are not merely theoretical: they correspond to known, actively tracked CVEs across multiple attack classes.
We characterize the weakness \emph{classes} in those records; we neither attempted nor observed exploitation on the tested devices, and any realized impact would remain conditional on device-specific deployment.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work}
(i)~\emph{Sampling and detection}: the controlled set covers ten devices, and retrospective detection relies on keyword filtering, which overcounts genuine transfers and whose recall is unmeasurable without ground truth; retrospective counts are therefore upper bounds, with the controlled experiments as the internally valid complement.
(ii)~\emph{Temporal comparability}: the Mon(IoT)r traces predate our captures by several years, so cross-dataset differences reflect ecosystem change as much as a single snapshot; we read agreement across both datasets (sub-threshold entropy, broadly offered legacy suites, long certificate lifetimes) as structural rather than transient.
(iii)~\emph{Passive perspective}: we infer risk from passive artifacts and do not validate negotiated-suite enforcement, on-device chain validation, or signature checking; a non-extractable certificate is not proof of absent authentication.
(iv)~\emph{Exposure, not exploitation}: the CVE mapping is exposure-linked rather than exploit-validated.
Future work follows directly from these bounds: multi-signal update detection (payload size, DNS, timing, learned classifiers) on a larger, longitudinally repeated testbed; active cipher-negotiation and certificate-validation probing; and end-to-end demonstration of downgrade-driven update substitution under our threat model.
